**What failed, and what it changed**

Three failures during Day 1 were real and changed the design of the code. They
are recorded because the brief asks for what failed, not only what works.

1. **`coerce_frame` silently dropped undeclared columns before the leakage audit
   ran.** `test_r09_is_enforced_on_the_write_path` caught it: a frame handed in
   with an `occupancy_count` column was written *successfully*, because
   coercion restricted output to schema columns first. A silent drop is worse
   than a refusal — the caller believes the field was exported and nothing
   records that it was not. Fixed by auditing the **incoming** frame in
   `write_dataset` before coercion.
2. **Five GT/OBS field-name collisions**, each needing its own decision rather
   than a blanket exemption:
   `D19.peak_w`/`min_w` vs `D13.peak_w` (per-interval vs per-cycle peak — a join
   between them would be nonsense) renamed to `interval_peak_w`/`interval_min_w`;
   `D25.sex`/`work_pattern`/`dwelling_type` vs `D05`/`D08`/`D02` renamed to
   `reported_*` (they are participant self-reports; MIMIC's own column names are
   preserved in the `mimic_field` crosswalk, so coding fidelity is unchanged);
   `participant_id` added to `SHARED_JOIN_KEYS` with a stated reason (it is
   MIMIC's own UUID and is the legitimate D25↔D26 join).
3. **`np.sum` and manual accumulation loops are order-sensitive on this machine**
   at the 1e-9 to 1e-7 level, measured, while CPython 3.12's builtin `sum` is
   not (it uses Neumaier compensation). A 7e-9 drift is invisible in a plot and
   fatal to a byte-identical hash, and the hot loops are exactly where numba
   compiles manual accumulation. Added `baile_nua/reduce.py` as the only
   sanctioned path for cross-entity reductions: sorted key order **and**
   `math.fsum`, so a feeder total cannot change with how its homes were
   partitioned across workers. My first attempt to *demonstrate* the instability
   used values that were too easy and the test correctly rejected them twice
   before I found a case that holds.

# Day 7 addendum — NC-D2-03: a check that can actually detect a 10% bias

The 10% shift is unchanged, no band was widened, and `marginal_acceptance` is
untouched. What changed is that the fault is now pointed at a check with the
power to see it, and that every control's magnitude is justified by a power
analysis rather than chosen.

**The power analysis (D-026).** The single-realisation band's deterministic
resolution is **12.82%** — 34.3 households — and the planted 10% shift moves
26.8 households, giving max |z| 2.1332 against a critical 2.7344. On a genuine
random draw of 1,092 households the same check has **42.6% power** at 10%,
needs 20% for 95%, and would need **3,664 households** to reach 95% power at
10%. Cobh has 5,257; the scaled town has 1,092. So 1,092 households do not
carry the information to resolve a 10% shift whatever check is applied: the
magnitude was the problem, not the care taken in applying the check. The
zero-shift row of the power curve gives 0.0595 against a declared α of 0.05,
which is the calibration check that lets the rest of the curve be trusted.

**What made a fix possible, and it is not more seeds.** The generator is
**deterministic** in this marginal: across 40 independent seeds, in both the
correct and the shifted arm, the across-run standard deviation of the
household-size shares is **1.7e-16**. It reproduces its control totals exactly,
and so does household type; age band and sex do vary. The acceptance band is
the multinomial sampling error of a 1,092-household *draw*, about 0.013 in
proportion near p = 0.25, while the generator's own run-to-run error is zero —
so the band is roughly **28× wider than the precision of the thing it tests**,
and a bias comfortably inside it is still an unambiguous defect. The band is
not wrong; it is the right band for "could a town this size look like this by
chance" and the wrong band for "is the generator biased".

**The ensemble check.** `ensemble_marginal_acceptance` tests the ensemble mean
of K runs at the generator's own measured precision — standard error
`max(sd/√K, half a household / n)`, Bonferroni **t** band on K−1 df. K = 20,
minimum 4, and the minimum has two independent derivations that agree: the
information requirement (3,664/1,092 = 3.36) and the measured power and
false-positive rates bootstrapped over 40 correct and 40 shifted runs. 20
rather than 4 because the band is a t quantile — 6.895 at K = 4 against 3.074
at K = 20 — so the check pays 13% for estimating its own variance instead of
152%, and keeps teeth on the marginals that do vary.

In households: the single-realisation band fires at **34.3**, the ensemble
check at **1.5**, and the planted shift moves **26.8**. Which is exactly why
one cannot see it and the other cannot miss it. Measured power **1.0**,
false-positive rate **0.0** — both exact rather than Monte Carlo, because with
zero across-run variance every draw of K runs gives the identical statistic.

**The false-positive control changed the design (D-027).** Taking "test the
ensemble mean against SAPS" literally **rejects a correct population** at every
K from 4 upwards: max |z| 54.9 against a t band of 6.90. The deliberate
person-total repair sits about 55 standard errors from raw SAPS once the
standard error shrinks to the generator's precision, and it is *larger* than
the fault being planted. So the ensemble tests the generator against the target
the declared procedure produces, and that target's deviation from SAPS is
bounded separately at 2.7344 (currently 1.9827). Two questions, two references.
Without the false-positive control I would have built a check that fired on
everything and called it sensitive.

**The audit (I-034).** 18 audited, 16 deterministic and 2 statistical; none
below its own resolution. The thinnest is NC-D2-04 at 1.13×, deliberately so
and now flagged in the report. T10 was audited although it is an E1-6 criterion
rather than a control, because a planted effect below its test's resolution
would make T10 pass for the wrong reason — its margin is 8.1×.

**A gap found on the way.** `tests/test_negative_controls.py` imported
`controls_day1` and `controls_day2` and nothing else, so **eight of seventeen
controls had never been in the pytest suite** — every Day 3 to 6 control
existed only behind `baile-nua controls run`. Nothing failed, because an
unregistered control is simply absent. Import lists are now replaced by
discovery shared between the CLI and the suite, and a test asserts every
controls module on disk is registered. The discovery's first pattern was itself
stale — it matched `register(NegativeControl(` on one line and missed the two
modules that split it across two — which the same test caught.

**Gate verdict: PASS, 8 of 8.** E1-3 is met because every control is now
rejected by its intended test for its recorded reason, not because anything was
relaxed. 251 tests, 1 skipped.

## What the suite is

`baile_nua/plausibility/` — nine declared checks, each carrying the quantity it
measures, the registered source it is held to, how the reference is read from
it, the tolerance and **why that tolerance**, plus a function that extracts the
measured value from a *written package* rather than from the simulation, so a
check cannot test the model against itself.

It reports **two counts, never one**: how many checks passed, and how many of
those were against a published reference. "6 of 6 evaluable checks pass" is not
the same claim as "5 of 9 families are sourced at all", and a suite reporting
only the first would read as coverage it does not have.

| Outcome | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `PASS` / `FAIL` | inside or outside the tolerance band |
| `NOT_EVALUABLE` | no reference *and* no declared band — nothing to compare |
| `NO_DATA` | the package lacks what the check needs |
| `sourced` | a separate field: does the tolerance trace to a published file? |

### What the check found: no overnight taper (I-044)

Real Irish domestic demand declines through the small hours because people go
to bed — LP1 falls from 0.77× the daily mean at midnight to 0.45× at 04:00. The
simulated town is flat: 0.44× at midnight, 0.46× at 04:00, every hour from
00:00 to 06:00 within 0.03 of the others. It gets the evening exactly right
(peak hour 18:00, matching LP1) and the night wrong, which points at
late-evening activity decay and the appliance standby floor rather than at the
physics: the town drops to a constant baseload the moment the day's activities
end instead of winding down.

Recorded as a **declared failure**, not absorbed. The suite reports FAIL, the
artefact records it, and `KNOWN_FAILURES` names the check and the issue so a new
failure still breaks the suite — and so does this one starting to pass, which is
what stops an entry outliving its defect (D-040). The fix belongs to week 2 step
4's behaviour work.

### Three things that had to be got right

Two of them only after getting them wrong, and both would have produced a
confident wrong number rather than an error:

**The export view.** D19 holds **four** configurations of the same energy —
`home_15min`, `home_30min`, `compliance_only` and a pre-summed

**The timing statistic.** LP1 2022's *annual* peak quarter-hour is 2022-12-27 at

### Demand is built the other way round from DEAP, deliberately

DEAP derives hot water demand from **floor area**, after SAP 10. This model
does not. U25 asks for draw events, and the activity engine already simulates
showering, bathing, washing up and cooking with HETUS-derived durations — so a
draw is generated from the activity that causes it, and DEAP's figure is kept
as the thing the result is **judged against** rather than the thing assumed.
That is the difference between a model that reproduces a reference by
construction and one that can disagree with it.

The shower is the part that is not assumed: DEAP's 6 L/min minimum times the
duration the activity engine drew. Bath capacity and tap flow rates DEAP takes
from manufacturers' product data and publishes no default for, so those stay
declared `[A]` with the U30 search recorded beside them.

### Gas cooking has no reference, and the chain says so

DEAP excludes cooking from its boundary and PxStat has nothing, so rather than
invent an annual figure, the model takes the useful cooking energy the
appliance layer has already sized and re-delivers it at a gas burner's
pan-side efficiency. The declared assumption is then a **single ratio** —
roughly two to one on the hob — rather than a new energy from nothing.

### Three errors the tests caught

**A draw is not referenced to the tap.** 48 litres delivered at 40 °C from a
60 °C tank carries **1.672 kWh**, and all of it leaves the tank: 28.8 litres
of 60 °C water falling 50 K. The first version multiplied the tank-side volume
by the *delivered* rise instead and recorded 1.003 kWh — understating hot water
by 40%. The tank-side mixing was right, so the two sides disagreed; there is
now an energy-balance assertion inside the step that catches it at the point
it is made rather than leaving it to the daily closure invariant.

**An immersion is a backup, not a fallback.** The reheat branch fell through to
the immersion whenever the boiler coil was unavailable, which would have put
electric immersion load into midwinter in every home whose boiler was briefly
off. DEAP 4.6 is specific: supplementary electric heating is *disregarded* when
it is only backup, and *assumed in summer* when running the boiler would also
heat the house. It is now an explicit `immersion_permitted` decision by the
caller. Found because a draw-side energy balance came out 0.07% short — the
immersion had quietly put heat back in.

**An electric shower must not be charged twice.** DEAP: an electric shower
*"uses electricity directly rather than from the water heating system"*. The
appliance layer already models it as a load, so the cylinder must see nothing,
and a test pins that the tank only cools by its standing loss.

Modules: `physics/dhw.py` (draw events, single-node tank, thermostat,
immersion, PV diverter) and `physics/boiler.py` (Table 4b efficiency, interlock
penalty, gas cooking, itemised meter total). **31 tests pass.** Engine wiring
is the next step.

**Nothing was fitted to get there.** Every number is a DEAP table row.

Hot water itself: 2,243 kWh/year useful, 400 kWh/year of standing loss, 64,488

### Five integration faults the wiring exposed

Each would have produced a plausible-looking wrong number rather than an error:

| | What would have happened |
| --- | --- |
| The immersion was **already an appliance** | Every immersion charged twice, for two different lots of hot water |
| A **failed immersion** became a no-op once it left the appliance list | A simulated disruption doing nothing; it now reaches the cylinder, which for an immersion-fed home means no hot water |
| Gas cooking had to **suppress** the electric load | Cooking counted as electricity *and* gas |
| **LPG and district heating** had no hot water at all — 10 dwellings of 1,092 | Found by the town's own fuel census; both are covered by the document already transcribed (Table 4b says "including LPG", Section 4.5 gives the district rule) |
| The coil input **already includes** making up the standing loss | Standing loss and draws both counted twice |

And one the measurement caught: hot water came out at about **800
litres/household/day**. DEAP's 6 L/min was being charged across the whole
`shower_bath` episode — which is time in the bathroom and runs to 25 minutes —
making 150-litre showers, 70–85% of all hot water. Separating the running time
from the episode brings it to 69 L/person/day. The shower duration stays a
declared assumption because DEAP publishes the flow rate and not the duration,

target instead of leaving it unchecked.

## U22 — comfort-taking (9 Oct 2026)

DBERNG01 says something a thermostat cannot do: within every dwelling type,
measured gas varies only **4 to 7%** across BER bands, and A–B rated detached
houses burn **more** gas than F–G ones, while varying 84% across dwelling
types. Measured consumption tracks dwelling **size**, not fabric quality. D-022
recorded that E1 deliberately did not wire this into the thermostat, treating
the flatness as a finding to report. U22 says build it — as behaviour, never as
a physics adjustment.

### The mechanism

Three COM-B terms with different drivers, because a single knob fitted to the
flatness would reproduce the flatness and tell us nothing:

| term | driver |
| --- | --- |
| **Opportunity** | cost per degree, from the dwelling's specific heat loss in W/K/m² |
| **Capability** | affordability, from income quintile, tenure and economic status |
| **Motivation** | the existing `comfort` trait, left as it was |

The falsifiable consequence is that **prebound is a cost response, not a fabric
response**. The same leaky dwelling at 4.0 W/K/m²:

| household | setpoint | heating hours | bedrooms heated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| quintile 1, local authority | **−1.50 K** | 71% | **44%** |
| quintile 5, owner, no mortgage | **−0.00 K** | 100% | 100% |

No fabric correction can produce that difference, which is what makes the
mechanism arguable rather than a fitted offset.

### What the ablation found

| arm | BER spread, gas at meter | ratio to published | mean indoor temp |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| off | 107.8% | 1.632 | 17.82 °C |
| **declared strength** | **75.2%** | 1.446 | 17.37 °C |
| ×2 | 50.9% | 1.294 | 16.99 °C |
| ×3 | **45.1%** | 1.227 | 16.83 °C |
| DBERNG01 | **4 to 7%** | 1.0 | — |

It moves the model 30 points towards the data at its declared strength, and
**saturates at 45% — it does not reach the published flatness at any strength.**

The binding constraint is not the one I predicted. I expected prebound to hit
PL-TEMP-01's temperature floor; mean indoor temperature only falls to 16.8 °C,
comfortably inside the band, and it is the mechanism's own clip bounds that
saturate.

A third term was added mid-measurement because the sweep showed the first two
saturating at 46%: **closing rooms**. The project's own description had it and I
had under-built it — *"heated warmer, longer and in more rooms"* — and real
Irish prebound is heating one room, not turning the whole house down a degree.
Abandoning a zone removes its losses instead of scaling them, which is the only
thing that can flatten consumption across a tenfold fabric spread. It bought
3 points: the sleeping zone is a quarter of emitter capacity and the two zones
are coupled through `r_zz`, so an unheated bedroom still draws heat from the
living space.

### What it points at, and what was not done

The residual spread **is** the modelled fabric range — 0.63 to 6.49 W/K/m², a
factor of ten. The likeliest missing term is **retrofit**: the model derives
fabric from construction era and BER band, and Ireland's old stock has been
substantially insulated under public programmes while its band label often
reflects the dwelling as built. That would make the real heat-loss spread much
narrower than the modelled one, which is exactly the discrepancy, and would
explain the 1.45× overshoot on old stock as well (I-048).

That is **not** tuning physics to absorb behaviour, which U22 forbids. It is a
question about whether the fabric model is right for retrofitted dwellings, and
it belongs to the week 3 fabric work beside CD488 and F2063.

**The strength was not fitted.** DBERNG01 is the declared calibration axis so
fitting would have been permitted — but the mechanism cannot reach the target at
any strength, so a fitted number would buy the appearance of calibration and a
kelvin of indoor temperature and nothing else (D-049). The declared amplitudes
stand and the sweep is published beside them.

### The held-out half cannot be run yet (I-049)

U22's validation condition is the gradient between 1967–99 and post-2005 stock.
Neither slice spans it:

| | E1 estate | mixed-age slice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1967–99 | 4 dwellings | 13 |
| post-2005 | 4 | **0** (max build year 1995) |

So the calibration half of U22 is done and the held-out half is blocked by the
slice, not the mechanism. A gradient across the slice's three old bands is in
the artefact for completeness and is explicitly marked as not testing the
claim. Cobh has both ends — the town's mean build year is 1974 — so it is a
selection problem, and adding a construction-period coverage constraint to the
U23 selector is the same kind of change U29 made for the people band.

### And a high-severity error the ablation caught (I-047)

The ablation's first run came back with a gas ratio of **2.11** on the mixed-age
slice against 0.83 on the modern E1 estate. A model that overshoots only where
the dwellings are old is not making a random error.

**U25 had read the dwelling's build year into DEAP Table 4b, which is indexed by
the boiler's age.** A house built in 1975 was being given a 55%-efficient
floor-mounted boiler in 2022; it is on its second or third boiler. Modelling
replacement — uniform over a ~19-year service life, condensing from Ireland's
2008 Part L requirement — gives 0.80 to 0.83 for every construction era and
brings the ratio to 1.46.

It also retracts a claim I had made in the U25 write-up: that Table 4b's 55–83%
spread was an era confound U22 would have to separate comfort-taking from. It
is not. Plant efficiency is nearly flat across the stock, and the era difference
in the gas comparison is not the boiler. The original paragraph is left visible
in place with the correction attached.

**And fixing it moved the two populations in opposite directions**, which is the
most useful thing the correction produced:

| | E1 estate (84% A/B) | mixed-age slice (86.7% pre-1980) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| before the fix | 0.832 | 2.11 |
| **after** | **0.785** | **1.446** |

The model **under**-consumes on modern stock and **over**-consumes on old
stock, a factor of 1.84 between the two populations: its response to fabric is
about 1.8× steeper than the meters say. That is DBERNG01's flatness measured a
second and independent way, and it is why the boiler error mattered beyond its
own arithmetic — it was masking the modern-stock shortfall while inflating the
old-stock excess, so the two partially hid each other and neither population
looked badly wrong alone.

The E1 year's own figures after both changes: space-heat ratio 0.330, meter
ratio **0.785**, 57.1% of dwellings within ±30% of their cell, mean boiler
efficiency 0.820, energy closing at 4.3e-16, and 9 plausibility checks passing
with the one declared failure.

## Week 2 step 7 — C2 coupling and the 8.2 timing contract (10 Oct 2026)

**Done.** All six steps of design 8.2's interval order implemented, and the
coupling-lag test found two real defects.

The staleness margin is derived from a published source: LP1's largest change
between adjacent quarter-hours, 0.256 of its mean, at 07:15 on 7 March — the
morning rise. A margin measured from our own output would have the model
certifying itself.

**Defect 1 — the envelope was not a network envelope.** Built from
per-connection headroom only, a literal reading of the contract's wording,
twenty homes each inside their own 12 kW service limit collectively overloaded
a 40 kW feeder. C1 then extracted 1,213 kWh against C2's 908 *while causing
the violations*, because a 15-minute commitment is made before a
15-minute-old solve can reveal the overload. That is the gain from stale
information 8.2 says is impossible, and it appears only when the binding
element is upstream — a test at one operating point would have certified a
broken contract. Fixed by taking the minimum of the connection's headroom and
its share of the upstream element's. All three binding regimes now pass with
zero violations, and C1 holds the feeder to 30.6 kW where C2 reaches 39.4 kW
under the same 40 kW limit.

**Defect 2 — the tightening rate was per epoch.** C2's 1440 epochs a day
tightened up to fifteen times as hard as C1's 96 for the same physical
violations, so the two modes differed by epoch counting rather than by
staleness. Both rates are now per unit of time.

Violations are counted, never corrected: with a feeder limit the baseline alone
exceeds, both modes record the overload (1050 and 1036 violation-minutes,
agreeing to within one epoch) at an identical 6.47 kW peak.
